Chefchaouen

A blue dream in the Rif Mountains
Sep 1–3, 2026 · 2 nights

The Blue City

Founded in 1471 by Moulay Ali ben Rashid as a hilltop fortress to resist Portuguese incursions, Chefchaouen soon became a refuge for Muslims and Jews fleeing Spain and Andalusia after the 1492 Reconquista. These Andalusian exiles shaped the town's whitewashed-and-blue houses, red-tiled roofs and horseshoe arches.

Why is it blue? It's genuinely debated. The most beloved story credits Jewish refugees (often dated to the 1930s) who painted the walls blue to evoke the sky, heaven, and tekhelet — the sacred blue of prayer-shawl tassels that reminds the faithful of the divine. Historians treat this as cherished tradition rather than documented fact (early photos show only patchy blue, mostly in the Jewish quarter, with the saturated blue spreading later). Competing theories say the blue keeps homes cool or repels mosquitoes.

Our pace here: the slowest, most peaceful stretch of the trip — wander, photograph, hike to a sunset viewpoint, sip mint tea. See the itinerary →

Must-See Sights

◆ The Blue Medina

Endlessly photogenic indigo-washed lanes, draped doorways and flower pots — the heart of any visit.

Must-see

◆ Plaza Uta el-Hammam

The lively main square ringed by cafés beneath the kasbah walls — ideal for people-watching.

◆ The Kasbah & Gardens

The 15th-c. fortress with Andalusian gardens, an ethnographic museum and a tower with Rif views (~9am–6pm, ~60 MAD).

◆ Spanish Mosque Viewpoint

A ~30-min uphill hike to a hilltop mosque for the best sunset panorama over the blue city.

Must-see

◆ Grand Mosque

Beside the square, notable for its rare octagonal minaret (interior closed to non-Muslims; admire from outside).

◆ Ras el-Maa

A refreshing spring and small waterfall just outside the medina's gate, where locals gather.

◆ Bab el-Ain

The main medina gate — a classic framed entry into the old town.

◆ Akchour Waterfalls

A ~45-min drive away: a scenic day-hike to cascades and "God's Bridge," a natural rock arch.

✡️ Jewish Heritage

Andalusian roots & the blue tradition

✡ The Andalusian Founders

Sephardic Jews expelled from Spain after 1492 settled here alongside Muslim refugees, shaping the town's crafts, cuisine and architecture.

✡ The Historic Mellah

Locals say the blue walls were first confined to the Jewish quarter before spreading. You can still walk its lanes, though it's now residential with no functioning Jewish institutions.

✡ The Blue-Painting Tradition

The town's signature link to Jewish heritage and tekhelet — honestly a meaningful tradition and one theory rather than proven history.

✡ What Remains

The last Jewish family emigrated (to Israel) in 1968, so there's no living community today; the legacy survives as story, the mellah's streets, and the blue itself. Ask locally about the old Jewish cemetery — access can be limited.

A meaningful stop for us: wandering the blue lanes where Sephardic refugees once settled is itself the heritage experience here — there's little formal infrastructure, but the story is everywhere.

Experiences

◆ Photography

Soft morning light on the blue lanes is unmatched — doorways, stairways and cats are the iconic shots.

◆ Rif Specialties

Local goat cheese, mountain honey, olives and bissara (fava-bean soup).

◆ Leather & Wool Crafts

Handwoven wool blankets, djellabas and leather goods throughout the medina.

◆ Rooftop Mint Tea

Unwind at a terrace café with sweeping blue-and-mountain views.

Practical Tips

Very walkable but steep: the medina is compact yet hilly with uneven cobbles — wear sturdy shoes.

Shoot early: go out at sunrise for the best light and near-empty lanes.

Early-September weather: mild mountain air — warm sunny days, cooler evenings. Pack a light layer (our cool-down after the desert and Fes).

Dress modestly & plan day-trips: cover shoulders/knees; arrange Akchour or park excursions with a grand taxi or guide and start early.
